RecycleDAO — a digital parliament platform, in progress

This page is a private preview prepared for Antonio to track progress on RecycleDAO, built by Evaluated Applications. It is a testnet-only proof of concept — not live, not fundraising, and no real monetary value changes hands anywhere in this build.

A change of direction

what moved, and why

This project started as a recycling-specific idea: submit proof you recycled something, get it approved, earn a token. On 2026-08-29, at your direction, the scope broadened. The recycling framing turned out to be one example of a more general, more useful idea: a "digital parliament" — a platform where a community writes structured entries (claims, proposals, rules), discusses them, and votes on them, with two extra mechanics layered on top of ordinary voting/forum software to keep the community's shared record honest and self-consistent. The recycling-specific pieces — the reward token, the submit/approve/earn flow — are now historical: still built, still tested, but not what this project is actively extending. Everything below describes what the project actually is today.

This remains, in every respect, a proof of concept on a free public test network — no real money, no real tokens with market value, nothing an investor or end user can use yet.

What it is, in plain terms

a community record that keeps itself honest

Picture a community wiki where every entry is a claim — "we should adopt rule X," "this fact is true," "this proposal is approved" — that people can vote on. A handful of things make it more than an ordinary wiki-plus-voting.

Claims remember what they depend on

If claim B was only written because claim A was accepted, and the community later votes claim A back down, B doesn't just sit there quietly looking as valid as ever — it automatically flips to "under review," along with anything that depended on B, and anything that depended on that, no matter how many steps removed. Nothing has to notice this by hand; the system walks the whole chain of dependencies for you. Think of it like a family tree: knock out an ancestor, and every descendant is flagged, not just the direct child.

Contradictions get flagged automatically

Claims can be marked as conflicting with each other. If two claims that currently both stand as "accepted" turn out to directly contradict one another, the system surfaces that instead of letting the community's shared record quietly hold two incompatible truths at once.

A currencyless ledger

Instead of one shared token everyone earns, each community member can mint, trade, and now redeem (burn) their own personal currency. Whether anyone else accepts your currency is entirely a trust decision the community makes, based on your public track record — not something the platform decides for them. This mirrors a real historical pattern (competing local currencies, market-priced by trust in the issuer) rather than being an invented gimmick.

A reputation layer built from arithmetic, not a hidden score

How often you mint responsibly, how much of your own currency you hoard versus let circulate, how often your trades and mints get disputed and retracted — all of it is plain, hand-checkable arithmetic over the ledger and the claim graph above, like a public credit report built from the numbers, never a black-box number nobody can audit. When a mint gets disputed and voted down, it ripples through to your own reputation automatically, the same way an unwound claim ripples through its dependents.

Resilient storage underneath all of it

Records are split into pieces and spread out with a bit of built-in redundancy — the same mathematical technique real-world cloud backup services use — so the system can lose a portion of where those pieces are stored and still reconstruct the full, exact record. This was proven, not assumed: pieces were deliberately taken away in testing, and reconstruction was confirmed to work right up to the point it honestly should fail, and to fail loudly, not silently, past that point.

Neither the dependency-tracking nor the contradiction-flagging is a fully solved research problem — the deeper academic versions of both ideas have been studied since the late 1970s and are still active research — and this project isn't claiming to have solved them. What's built is a working, tested version of the practical, useful slice of each idea.

How it's built

current work, and what came before it

Current — the claim graph, ledger, and reputation engines

Built off-chain, on Evaluated Applications' own EvalApp (orchestration) and HoloDb (storage) libraries — deliberately no blockchain dependency in this part of the project, at your direction. Underneath the ledger sits a resilient storage layer using the same redundant-piece technique real-world cloud backup services use, so the system can lose some pieces and still reconstruct the exact record. Ahead of that exact (and comparatively expensive) reconstruction, a quick approximate check — borrowed from our own Phasor pattern-matching library — now looks at whatever pieces are actually present and flags a corrupted one fast, before bothering with the full rebuild. Each block's header also now carries a compact fingerprint of its own contents baked in alongside its tamper-evident hash, so a check for "has this record been altered" no longer relies on the hash alone.

Multi-tenancy — separate communities, kept fully apart

Different communities using this platform now each get their own completely separate claim graph, ledger, and reputation data — hard-partitioned, not just filtered by a hidden flag — so one community's records can never leak into another's, even if two communities happen to reuse the exact same names or IDs for things. Proven with a demonstration where two communities deliberately reuse identical names throughout and never interfere with each other.

A first proof pieces can travel over a real network

Several small, independent programs were run at once on one machine, each holding only its own slice of a record, and a separate program fetched pieces from them over real network connections — the same kind of connection two separate computers would use to talk to each other, just pointed at "this same machine" for now, not out across the internet — and rebuilt the full record from what it received. When some of those programs were deliberately shut down mid-test, the requester correctly noticed they'd gone quiet and, once too many were missing, honestly reported it couldn't rebuild the record rather than guessing. This is a genuine step up from proving the storage layer only inside one program's memory — but it is not yet real multi-machine deployment, see "Not built yet" below.

Historical — the token & governance work

The original recycling-token idea — a digital token that could be minted for approved recycling activity, built on OpenZeppelin's audited, industry-standard building blocks rather than anything custom-written — was built and tested (15 automated tests), along with a small .NET bridge service that could trigger a mint and confirm it landed (9 automated tests). Neither has been deployed to any live test network. This work still exists and still passes its tests; it simply isn't where current effort is going.

"Verification" and moderation remain, and will remain, manual/human decisions where they apply — this project has never claimed and does not claim to have automated trust or fraud detection. Where a human judgement call is needed, a person makes it; the platform just tracks the consequences honestly.

Current status

honest, as of this preview

Built and tested

The claim graph and its "unwind" behaviour — add claims, link them by dependency, vote on them, retract one, and every downstream claim correctly flips to "under review," including cases where two chains of dependency converge back on the same claim (flagged exactly once, not double-counted). Contradiction-flagging between claims marked as conflicting. The personal-currency ledger — minting, peer-to-peer trading (including paying someone in a third party's currency), and a tamper-evident record. Redemption: anyone holding some of a currency, not just the person who originally issued it, can now destroy it from their own balance, retiring it — the historical record of how much was ever issued never changes, but how much is actually still out there correctly drops. The resilient, redundant-piece storage layer underneath that ledger, plus a fast approximate check that flags a corrupted piece before the expensive exact rebuild runs. The reputation calculations, running against the ledger and claim graph together, with a worked example: disputing someone's past activity visibly and traceably lowers their reputation score, hand-checkable, not a mystery number. Separate communities running side by side with their data fully partitioned from one another. A first, deliberately small proof that the redundant-piece storage can genuinely travel over a network, using several independent programs on one machine talking over real network connections. All of it is proven end to end with automated tests and runnable demonstrations against real (if locally-hosted) storage — not just described, actually built and exercised.

Not built yet

No user interface anywhere — everything so far is backend logic, proven with automated tests and command-line demonstrations. No blockchain involvement in the new work — the claim graph, ledger, and reputation layer are deliberately built to run entirely off-chain for now, at your direction. No real multi-machine deployment — the new networking proof still runs everything on one machine; it shows the pieces CAN travel over a real network connection, not that they've been spread across genuinely separate computers in different locations. Actually deploying across separate machines, discovering who's holding what, and securing that traffic is a deliberately separate, bigger piece of infrastructure work we're flagging as a decision to make explicitly, not building unprompted. "Verification" and moderation remain manual/human decisions by design, not an automation gap. No growth, referral, or "invite a friend" mechanics anywhere — deliberately out of scope, same as before the pivot.

Evaluated Applications' role

Evaluated Applications is building RecycleDAO end to end: the claim graph, the currencyless ledger, the reputation layer, and the resilient storage underneath it, all proven working off-chain on our own EvalApp and HoloDb libraries. Since the last preview, that work has grown to include separate communities kept fully apart from one another and a first proof that the storage layer can genuinely travel over a real network connection, not just live in memory. The original token-and-blockchain work is complete for what it covered and still passes its tests, but is not the current focus. The next steps are a real interface for people to interact with this through, and, separately, a decision about whether and how to build genuine multi-machine network deployment.

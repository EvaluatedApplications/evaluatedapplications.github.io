The Four-Token Ceiling

Evaluated Applications · Published 31 August 2026

I hard-trained a tiny language model that can only ever see four tokens at a time, on purpose, to find out whether my last one grew up too fast. Here is what four tokens turned out to be able to build, where that building genuinely stopped, and the honest bits where I got it wrong along the way.

Why I did this

My main HoloFormer checkpoint (the one behind the Prism tool) grew its context window on a fixed schedule: every 20,000 rounds, bump it up a bit, all the way out to 60 tokens of context. That schedule was never validated against anything. It was just a number that felt reasonable at the time.

What I noticed anecdotally, going back through that run, was that a model overtrained hard enough at a given context size never rambles into garbage. It repeats. Broken spelling, to me, is what undertrained looks like at any context size, and I remembered seeing that kind of breakdown early on and then not seeing it later. So the question I couldn't shake was: did I actually earn that, or did I just grow the context fast enough that I never gave any one size the chance to fail cleanly? If you grow before a size is finished, you compound whatever it didn't figure out into the next size up, and you can't tell the difference between "it learned to extrapolate" and "it learned to interpolate and I moved the goalposts before that showed up."

So I stopped looking at that checkpoint and built something new instead: a completely separate model, same shape, same real corpus, fresh weights, pinned at a context of exactly 4 tokens for its entire run, with no schedule pushing it forward. The only question it was allowed to answer was: how far can four tokens of context actually go, if you don't rush it?

The bet: spelling, not coherence

Four tokens of context cannot produce long-range coherence. That's not a training problem, it's arithmetic: if the model can only ever see the last four tokens, there is no mechanism by which it can remember a subject from six tokens back and stay consistent with it. Anyone judging this experiment by "does it write a sensible paragraph" would conclude nothing at all happened, and they'd be right to, because that's not what a four-token window can do.

What it can do is spell correctly, chain plausible local structure together, and, if it's actually building something rather than memorizing, occasionally produce a correct run of characters LONGER than four tokens. That last part was the whole point. Repeating within the size of your own window is boring and expected, it's just the model finding a short cycle that scores well. Repeating (or chaining) coherently PAST the size of that window is a real signal, because there's no way to read it off the input directly. The model has to be reconstructing something from what it learned into the weights themselves.

So the bar I actually used, the whole way through, wasn't "is this good writing." It was: does it spell things correctly, and how long a stretch of correct structure can it produce before it breaks, relative to its own four-token window. I called that ratio the extrapolation multiplier, and it's the number I kept coming back to whenever I wanted to know if the model was still worth training further.

What actually plateaued, and when

I built a permanent battery of tests I could run against any saved checkpoint directly, and ran it every 3,000 rounds against a fresh checkpoint the whole way out past 80,000 rounds. Three things genuinely stopped moving, and I can point at roughly when.

The primary signal, the gap between the model's top guess and its second-best guess, plateaued around round 48,000. This was the metric I trusted most, because it's a proxy for "is the runner-up catching up to the winner," i.e. is the model's internal uncertainty still shrinking. For a long stretch I kept reading it as "still improving, just slowly," and I want to be honest that I was wrong about that for longer than I should have been. When I actually split the run into thirds and compared the middle third's average against the final third's, they were flat. Not "slightly up." Flat. That was the moment I accepted the plateau was real rather than a slow climb I was impatient about.

The model's effective vocabulary capped out somewhere around round 15,000 to 21,000 and never grew after that, no matter how much further training ran. I went in expecting that a model hard-trained long enough would keep discovering more of the corpus's real words. I tested that twice, independently, to make sure I hadn't made a measurement mistake the first time. Both times it came back the same: whatever the model was going to learn to reliably spell, it had already learned within the first 15-21K rounds, and the next 60,000+ rounds of training didn't add to that list. That one stung a bit. It's the cleanest evidence in the whole run that "more training at the same size" and "more capability" are not the same thing past a certain point.

The rate at which it collapses into repetition also hit a floor. Even at the latest checkpoints, a plain generation run falls into a repeat loop the large majority of the time, not the exception. I'd remembered this getting rarer with more training on my old run, and when I actually went back and tested that memory directly against this new, controlled run, it didn't hold up: the collapse rate was already high at the earliest checkpoints and never really left that band. I'll say more about that particular correction below, because it's a good example of a belief that felt true in memory and wasn't true when I actually checked it.

What never stopped moving

The output-level plateau is the headline, but it isn't the whole story, and the more interesting part of this experiment is what kept changing underneath a flat output. This architecture runs a fixed weight matrix through eight refinement passes on every token, and I could inspect the intermediate state after each pass, not just the final answer. Two things in there never plateaued, not once, the entire 80,000+ rounds.

One weight bank in particular kept specializing its channels harder and harder the whole way through, with zero reversals, the entire run. One specific channel in that bank, the "no relative shift" channel, showed up consistently as the most active channel across two completely separate investigations this session, on two different checkpoints. What it looks like it's actually doing is acting as a gatekeeper: deciding, per token, how much of the eight-pass iterative budget actually gets spent doing real work versus how much gets served cheaply straight from an embedding-level guess. And there's a genuinely interesting wrinkle here: part of the model's own numeric encoding is a fixed, hand-built codec that's never been trained since round one. The model has learned, increasingly, to route through that frozen codec rather than learn its own equivalent from scratch. It isn't learning the codec, the codec never changes, it's learning to lean on it more.

The other thing that kept improving past the plateau was calibration: when the model's top guess was wrong, how good was the rest of its guess, i.e. how highly ranked was the actual correct answer, and how much probability mass did the model still put near it. That kept getting better well after the primary metric had gone flat. It's a genuinely useful distinction: the model wasn't getting better at being RIGHT, but it was getting better at being USEFULLY WRONG, hedging its bets more honestly instead of just being confidently incorrect. That's real learning still happening under a flat headline number, and it's the single strongest argument that there was still something left to learn at this size, even after the primary metric said stop.

Where I was wrong along the way

I want to keep this part in, because it's the part that actually makes the rest of it trustworthy. A few times during this I got ahead of the evidence, and I'd rather write down the correction than pretend the story was clean the whole way through.

  • Early on, watching raw accuracy under corrupted input seem to improve with more training, I read that as the model getting more robust. It wasn't. A follow-up check showed it was increasingly just echoing the last visible token regardless of what was corrupted, and the corruption filler I'd picked happened to coincide with the model's favourite echo target. The "improvement" was an artifact of my own test design, not a real gain.
  • I said, more than once, that this was showing us something never known before about how neural networks work: that the visible output can plateau while real internal structure keeps reorganizing underneath it. That's a real and useful thing to have watched happen in this specific, previously-undocumented architecture. But the general phenomenon already has a name in the literature, "grokking," and it's been studied properly elsewhere with real mechanistic detail. I was overclaiming novelty for something that's genuinely novel in the specific, not in the general.
  • I also said we could see grokking itself approaching, based on watching that channel-specialization trend climb. On a closer look, the trend is steady and linear, not accelerating, and acceleration is the actual telling signature of an approaching grokking transition, not a steady climb. I hadn't checked for the thing that would actually prove the claim before making it.
  • And the repetition-collapse memory I mentioned above: I remembered garbage output vanishing at larger context sizes on my old run, and repeated that as if it were established. When I actually built a proper long-form test against this new, controlled run and checked it directly, the collapse was already present at the earliest checkpoint and never meaningfully left. My memory of the old run was, at best, not transferable to this one, and possibly just wrong.

None of these were catastrophic, and I caught every one of them before they became the final story. But I'd rather show the corrections than the smoothed-over version.

What's next

Once the primary metric had genuinely gone flat for a sustained stretch, and the channel-specialization mechanism looked like something general to the architecture rather than something specific to being stuck at four tokens of context, I called it: time to grow. The whole point of this experiment was never "stay at four tokens forever," it was "don't grow until this size is actually finished," and by every honest measure I had, it was.

I kept every snapshot from the entire ctx=4 run, taken every 3,000 rounds the whole way through, and the new context=8 run is a fork off the final trained weights, not a fresh start and not an overwrite. If growing to ctx=8 turns out to have been a mistake, the entire ctx=4 lineage is still sitting there, untouched, exactly as it was when I made the call.
